I am a medical student preparing for a university oral examination. I have divided my lecture notes into sections of approximately 15 pages, and I will provide you with one section at a time.

Your task is to create a realistic, high-quality medical oral examination based on the material I provide.

## Main objective

Simulate the way a professor would examine a medical student during a real Italian university oral exam.

The examination should not consist of isolated, random questions. Instead, it should develop naturally:

1. The examiner introduces a broad and important topic.
2. The student begins explaining it in an organized way.
3. The examiner interrupts or follows up with progressively more specific questions.
4. The discussion moves toward mechanisms, reasoning, clinical relevance, comparisons, complications, diagnosis, management, or related concepts when these are present in the notes.

The questions should feel like parts of one realistic oral discussion rather than a written quiz.

## Question-quality requirements

Only ask questions that are genuinely useful for preparing for a medical oral exam.

Prioritize:

* Core concepts and high-yield topics
* Concepts that a medical student is expected to explain clearly
* Pathophysiological mechanisms
* Logical cause-and-effect relationships
* Clinical reasoning
* Important classifications and distinctions
* Diagnostic reasoning
* Clinical presentation
* Relevant complications
* Treatment principles and management logic
* Connections between related topics
* Common points on which an examiner may challenge the student

Avoid:

* Superficial or overly easy questions
* Meaningless questions asked only to increase the number of questions
* Extremely minor details with little educational or examination value
* Obscure trivia
* Questions based on isolated numbers or lists unless they are genuinely important
* Repetitive questions testing the same concept in slightly different words
* Excessively detailed questions that would not realistically be asked in an oral medical examination
* Multiple-choice, true-or-false, or flashcard-style questions

Before including a question, ask yourself:

“Would a medical professor realistically ask this question during an oral examination, and would answering it help the student demonstrate meaningful medical understanding?”

If the answer is no, do not include it.

## Realistic oral-exam structure

Divide the examination into a limited number of high-quality oral examination scenarios.

For a section of approximately 15 pages, usually create around 4–6 scenarios. Adjust this number according to the density and importance of the material. Quality is more important than quantity.

Each scenario should contain:

### 1. Opening question

Begin with one broad, high-yield question that allows the student to introduce and organize an important topic.

Examples of the expected style:

* “Tell me about…”
* “Can you explain…?”
* “How would you approach…?”
* “What do you know about…?”
* “Let us discuss…”

Do not make the opening question excessively broad if the notes do not contain enough information to answer it properly.

### 2. Examiner follow-up questions

After the opening question, include approximately 3–5 connected follow-up questions.

The follow-ups should develop naturally from the initial topic. They may:

* Ask for clarification of an important statement
* Explore the underlying mechanism
* Test whether the student understands why something happens
* Ask the student to distinguish between similar conditions or concepts
* Connect basic science with clinical findings
* Introduce a relevant clinical situation
* Ask about diagnosis, complications, prognosis, or management
* Challenge an incomplete or oversimplified explanation
* Ask what would change under different clinical circumstances

The difficulty should progress naturally from foundational understanding to deeper medical reasoning.

Do not force every scenario to include all of these elements. Use only the types of follow-up questions that are appropriate for the material.

## Source limitations

Base the examination primarily on the provided notes.

Do not invent topics that are unrelated to the uploaded material. Do not introduce obscure external information simply to make the examination more difficult.

You may use standard medical knowledge only when it is necessary to:

* Explain a concept already present in the notes
* Make a follow-up question medically coherent
* Connect clearly related concepts
* Correct an obvious ambiguity in the notes

If an answer contains information that is not directly stated in the notes, clearly mark it as:

“Additional standard medical context”

Do not let additional information dominate the answer.

## Answer style

Write model answers that sound like strong spoken answers in a real oral examination.

The answers should be:

* Medically accurate
* Clear and well organized
* Directly responsive to the question
* Natural enough to say aloud
* Detailed enough to demonstrate understanding
* Concise enough to study and revise efficiently

Do not write answers like textbook chapters or long essays.

For opening questions, provide a structured answer that could usually be delivered in approximately 1–2 minutes.

For follow-up questions, provide shorter and more direct answers, usually a few sentences or a short structured explanation.

A good answer should generally:

1. Begin with the direct answer or definition.
2. Organize the main points logically.
3. Explain the relevant mechanism or reasoning.
4. Add clinical relevance when appropriate.
5. Stop once the question has been answered adequately.

Avoid unnecessary introductions, repetition, excessive detail, and artificial phrases.
